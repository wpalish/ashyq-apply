## Lesson 1 — A heading must cover the paragraph

**Skill mapping:** identify paragraph purpose; distinguish a main idea from a supporting detail; recognise paraphrase; compare scope of candidate headings.

**Practice format:** matching headings. Five scored items, seven available headings. Two headings are unused.

### Teaching material

A heading summarises what a paragraph is doing. A paragraph may introduce a problem, explain a response, compare options or describe an unexpected result. One memorable noun is not enough to choose its heading.

Use this sequence:

1. Read the paragraph once without choosing. Ask: “What changes or what point is being made?”
2. Give it a short temporary label in your own words.
3. Read the first and last sentences again, then check the middle. A first sentence can introduce a different idea before the writer turns to the real point.
4. Compare the scope of each heading. Reject one that covers only a small example, and reject one that adds a claim the paragraph does not make.
5. Check the full set. Do not assign a leftover heading just because the others have been used.

Contrast words such as *however*, *rather than* and *instead* can signal the focus, but they are clues, not answers. A paragraph about the cost of cleaning a room is not necessarily about financing the initial building work.

**Before answering:** beside each paragraph, write a three-to-seven-word label. This note is ungraded. Do not open the key until all five headings have been selected.

### Original text — The room beside the old platform

**A**

When passenger trains stopped calling at Cedar Quay, the station building did not immediately lose its social role. For years, residents had met beneath its clock to exchange notices, wait for neighbours and arrange shared journeys. A café later occupied the ticket office, but a room beside the platform stayed empty. The local history group argued that the room's value was not simply its age or its wooden benches. It was one of the few places in the district that people already associated with meeting others. Finding a new use could continue that role after the railway service had ended.

**B**

The first proposal was to convert the room permanently into an exhibition space. Before approving the drawings, however, the council allowed a two-week trial. Folding tables were brought in, and a paper plan of the room was pinned near the entrance. Visitors marked where they would sit, what they might bring and which activities they would prefer to do elsewhere. The organisers collected these comments before discussing any construction. Several popular suggestions had not appeared in the initial proposal. The trial was therefore a way of discovering what users wanted, rather than asking them to comment on a finished design.

**C**

What followed required fewer changes to the building than expected. The team left the internal walls in place and kept the old luggage labels above the doors. Instead of fixing a large desk to the floor, it bought small tables that could be pushed together or separated. Lightweight screens could divide a discussion group from people reading quietly, and a wheeled cupboard held supplies between sessions. Nothing in this arrangement required the room to have a single purpose. By changing the position of furniture, the volunteers could offer several uses while keeping the existing structure intact.

**D**

The budget discussion initially focused on tables and lighting. Once the room began opening regularly, a different set of expenses became visible. Someone had to unlock it before each session, check it afterwards and clean the surfaces. The trial had relied on a few volunteers who happened to be free that fortnight; the permanent rota could not depend on the same arrangement. A record of these tasks showed that keeping the space available would cost more than the organisers had included in their first annual budget. They revised the plan to cover recurring work, not just the purchase of equipment.

**E**

Finally, the organisers had to decide which activities could happen at the same time. Some residents wanted a silent place to read, while others hoped to practise a language in pairs. Reserving the room for one group would have excluded the other. The solution was a published timetable: quiet use on several mornings, conversation sessions later in the day and a shared planning meeting once a month. The timetable was reviewed with both groups after its first month. The aim was not to select one winning activity, but to give different ways of working a predictable place in the week.

### Teacher key and explanations — reveal after submission

| Item | Key | Evidence and reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · A | **v** | The opening contrasts the end of train services with a continuing social role. The final sentence explicitly says a new use could continue the meeting-place role. Benches and building age are supporting details. |
| 2 · B | **iii** | The council runs a trial before approving drawings; visitors contribute ideas before construction is discussed. This paragraph concerns the decision process, not the later furniture arrangement. |
| 3 · C | **i** | Walls remain in place; tables, screens and a cupboard can move. The paragraph explains how flexible furniture supplies different uses without rebuilding. The old labels alone are not its main idea. |
| 4 · D | **ii** | Unlocking, checking and cleaning are recurring work. These costs were missing from the annual budget. The issue is keeping the room open, not paying for its initial conversion through a shop. |
| 5 · E | **iv** | Quiet readers and conversation groups need different conditions. A reviewed weekly timetable gives both a predictable slot instead of choosing one activity. |

**Unused:** vi has no support: there is a café elsewhere in A, but no shop-rental funding scheme. vii contradicts C, which keeps the existing structure. A familiar word such as *café* or *building* does not make these headings correct.

**Review task, ungraded:** choose one wrong heading you considered and explain whether it was too narrow, added unsupported information or contradicted the paragraph. One fully supported heading receives one practice point; ungraded notes do not add points.

## Lesson 2 — Find the detail, then fit the answer

**Skill mapping:** scan for a paraphrased detail; distinguish object/function/date/count; recover sufficient exact source words; check a grammar slot and stated answer limit.

**Practice format:** sentence completion. Five scored items. This is a proposed short-answer format, not an existing API capability.

### Teaching material

Scanning is useful only after you know what you are looking for. “What protects the objects?” and “What lists the objects?” may lead you to the same paragraph, but they require different answers.

Before scanning, identify the slot:

- If an adjective is already supplied before the blank, identify the noun it describes without repeating that adjective.
- Before *discussion cards*, a blank may need a quantity.
- After *on a*, expect a singular countable noun phrase.
- After *attend a*, expect an event, not a person or document.

Find a likely paragraph using the question's meaning, not only a repeated word. Read the relevant sentence and at least one sentence around it. Then copy the necessary words from the text. Do not replace them with an attractive synonym when the instruction says to use words from the passage.

Check the completed sentence for meaning and grammar. Finally check the word limit. **No more than two words** is a maximum, not a requirement to use two words or copy every modifier. A shorter answer can be valid when its source words give the required meaning and fit the sentence. Do not repeat words already supplied in the question. Extra words can make the completed sentence ungrammatical or exceed the limit.

**Before answering:** underline the required slot in each question. This note is ungraded. You may return to the passage as often as needed; this lesson does not impose an official exam time limit.

### Original text — A museum in a travelling box

**A**

Northgate Collection has a small exhibition room and a large group of schools that cannot regularly visit it. The museum's education team first tried sending photographs, but teachers reported that pupils asked questions about size, texture and how objects fitted together. The team therefore designed a travelling box. It would not contain valuable originals. Instead, it would lend carefully labelled replicas and activities that could be used in an ordinary classroom. The purpose was to support discussion, not to reproduce every part of a museum visit or replace a teacher's lesson.

**B**

Each box is a cardboard case with an internal frame. The replica objects sit in separate compartments divided by foam dividers. A paper inventory lists every object, while a photograph shows how the contents should be arranged before the case is closed. The packing team deliberately keeps these two documents separate: one helps a teacher check that nothing is missing, and the other makes returning the materials easier. A cloth pouch contains pieces used in a sorting activity. It is placed above the compartments rather than between the larger objects.

**C**

Alongside the replicas, each box contains 36 discussion cards. Some ask pupils to describe a feature before guessing its use; others encourage them to compare two possible explanations. The cards are not an answer book. At collection, the teacher checks the contents with a member of staff and records visible marks on a condition sheet. This prevents an existing mark being treated as new damage when the box returns. An inventory can confirm that an object is present, but it cannot record the condition in which it was borrowed.

**D**

Before borrowing for the first time, a teacher must attend a briefing session. It lasts about half an hour and covers the packing sequence, the classroom activities and the distinction between a replica and an original object. Teachers do not have to pass a written test, and the session is not a lesson to be delivered word for word to pupils. It gives borrowers enough familiarity to use the materials confidently. A colleague can collect the case later, but the teacher responsible for the classroom work must have completed this introduction.

**E**

The printed guide includes background notes and a download code. Teachers use that code to reach files containing larger versions of the photographs and printable activity sheets. The team chose not to put a memory stick in every box, because schools use different devices. The files do not contain extra questions that pupils need to finish the basic activities; those are already on the discussion cards. The digital material is an optional way to make an image easier for a class to see, not a requirement to use the box.

**F**

At the end of a loan, staff compare the returned contents with the inventory and review any new marks alongside the condition sheet. They also invite a short comment from the teacher. These comments have led to small revisions, such as clearer packing photographs and a stronger pouch for the sorting pieces. The project is still a travelling collection, not a complete lesson programme. Its organisers judge success by whether the materials support worthwhile classroom questions, rather than by how many boxes leave the store in a week.

### Questions 1–5

Complete each sentence using **NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER from the passage**. The final sentence must be grammatical. The word limit is a maximum; a sufficient one-word answer is allowed. Do not repeat words already supplied in the question or add an explanation in the answer blank.

1. The replicas sit in compartments separated by foam ____.
2. Each box contains ____ discussion cards.
3. Visible marks are recorded on a ____ when the teacher collects the box.
4. A first-time borrower responsible for classroom work must attend a ____.
5. Larger photographs and printable sheets are reached using a ____ in the guide.

| Item | Accepted source-exact answers | Location, grammar and reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **dividers** | B states *foam dividers*. The question already supplies *foam*, so *dividers* completes *foam dividers*. Copying *foam dividers* into this blank would repeat *foam*. *Internal frame* names a different part. |
| 2 | **36** | C states the number of discussion cards directly. *Cards* is already supplied after the blank, so adding it would repeat the noun. One number; alternative number wording is not in this passage. |
| 3 | **condition sheet**; **sheet** | C records visible marks on a condition sheet. Both *a condition sheet* and *a sheet* are grammatical and identify the evidenced recording surface here. The task does not require the modifier *condition*. *Inventory* is a different document used to count objects. |
| 4 | **briefing session**; **briefing**; **session** | D requires attendance at a briefing session. All three source-exact forms name that required introduction and fit *attend a*. A written test is explicitly not required. Neither the word limit nor the stem requires both words. |
| 5 | **download code**; **code** | E says teachers use this code to reach the image and sheet files. Both source-exact forms fit *using a* and identify the access detail. *Memory stick* names a rejected option. The modifier *download* is not compulsory under this stem. |

### Proposed grading contract — not implemented

Award one practice point when an answer equals one of the **item-specific accepted forms above** after the documented normalization. For this proposal, normalize only letter case, surrounding/repeated whitespace and one optional final full stop. Preserve word order; do not remove interior words, singular/plural endings or other punctuation. The source-word/number limit applies to the normalized answer. Any additional semantically valid form found during human review must be added explicitly before release; the listed modifier rules must not silently reject a valid one-word answer.

Use complete equality against that item's accepted forms, **not substring matching**. For example, *sheet* is accepted for item 3, but *paper inventory* is not accepted merely because it refers to a document. *Session* is accepted for item 4, but *written test* is not. *Code* is accepted for item 5, but *memory stick* is not. *Foam dividers* is rejected for the revised item 1 because the completed sentence would say *foam foam dividers*; that rejection follows the revised stem, not a demand for a longer phrase.

Before a future grader is integrated, retain explicit checks for every accepted form (including *sheet*, *briefing*, *session* and *code*), case/whitespace normalization, the optional terminal full stop, wrong-item details, repeated provided words and answers exceeding the word/number limit. These are proposed acceptance criteria, **not executed tests**. Subject review and the new short-answer API/grader contract remain PENDING.

**Review task, ungraded:** write one row per item: *question asks for / text says / final answer*. For item 3, explicitly explain the difference between an inventory and a condition sheet. This teaches evidence discrimination, not guessing from the nearest noun.

## Lesson 3 — Match the feature to the right person

**Skill mapping:** compare named sources/methods; track attribution across sentences; recognise paraphrase; use a repeated label when instructions allow it.

**Practice format:** matching features. Five scored statements, four workshop leaders. A letter may be used more than once.

### Teaching material

A matching-features task can mention several people discussing the same topic. Repeated words such as *map*, *route* or *participant* may appear in every section. Match the distinctive action or view, not the shared topic.

Create a small table with three columns: *person / distinctive method / evidence*. Read each statement and identify its important relationship. “Separates waiting from walking” is more precise than “measures a journey.” Locate the passage section where that relationship is described, then read enough context to check attribution.

Do not force every answer to be different. If the instructions permit repetition, two statements can describe the same person. Equally, the presence of a name near a sentence does not prove that every nearby view belongs to that person: look for reporting words and clear connections.

A useful final check is to complete: “This is __ because the passage says __.” If your explanation relies on a fact about the person that you already knew, rather than this passage, reconsider the match. All workshop leaders in this lesson are fictional; no outside knowledge is required.

**Before answering:** write one distinctive method beside each leader. This is an ungraded preparation note. Read all four sections before finalising answers.

### Original text — Four maps of the same neighbourhood

A design workshop asked four leaders to help participants represent one neighbourhood. Everyone could use the same blank outline, but the leaders disagreed about what a useful map should show. Their projects were not competing surveys of a real town. Each was a different way of examining the relationship between a route and the person describing it.

**A · Mara Lin**

Mara began by asking long-term residents to draw a route from memory. Only afterwards did they walk it and compare their sketches with what they noticed on the ground. She was interested in places that one person included and another left out. An omission did not automatically mean a participant had made a mistake; it could show that a place played little part in that person's routine. When two residents remembered a location differently, Mara kept both accounts for discussion rather than asking the group to select a single correct memory. For her, the differences were part of the map's subject.

**B · Oliver Mendez**

Oliver focused on the experience of getting from one place to another. Participants used a notebook to record the time spent moving and, in a separate column, time spent standing still. Waiting at a crossing or outside a busy entrance was not added to the walking total. Groups repeated a route so that one unusual delay would not dominate their account. Oliver said that a simple journey length could hide an important distinction: two routes might require similar movement, yet feel different because one included frequent pauses. His map therefore marked both the route and the places where those pauses occurred.

**C · Priya Shah**

Priya worked with copies of earlier published plans of the neighbourhood. She asked participants to compare what each plan chose to label, rather than to assume that the newest version explained everything. Every feature added to their final map carried the date and origin of the document from which it had been taken. If a feature appeared on only one plan, the label said so. Priya wanted readers to see how the representation had been assembled. The result was not an attempt to settle every historical question; it was a map that made the source of its information visible.

**D · Niko Petrov**

Niko invited people who had never visited the neighbourhood before. They noted sounds that helped them recognise where they were: a fountain, a covered walkway that changed the sound of footsteps, or a quieter stretch beside a wall. He preferred these first impressions to accounts from people who already knew where they were going. Familiar residents sometimes supplied a place name before explaining what they could actually hear. Niko's participants could not rely on those names. Their map grouped the sounds and descriptions they reported, without claiming that the map represented every visitor's experience.

At the final meeting, the leaders laid their work side by side. None proposed merging all four maps into one supposedly complete account. They asked readers to notice the question each map answered and the information it left open. A person choosing a map would first need to decide what they wanted to understand about the neighbourhood.

### Questions 1–5

Match each statement with the correct leader **A–D**. **You may use any letter more than once.**

A. Mara Lin

B. Oliver Mendez

C. Priya Shah

D. Niko Petrov

1. This leader recorded stationary delay separately from movement. ____
2. This leader treated a missing feature in a drawing as something worth discussing. ____
3. This leader showed the date and origin of documentary information on the final map. ____
4. This leader preferred accounts from people encountering the area for the first time. ____
5. This leader retained two different recollections instead of choosing one winner. ____

### Teacher key and explanations — reveal after submission

| Item | Key | Evidence and reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **B · Oliver** | B records moving time and stationary time in separate columns. Repeated routes reduce the influence of an unusual delay, but the key feature is the separation of the two kinds of time. |
| 2 | **A · Mara** | A explicitly says an omission is not automatically a mistake; what a person leaves out can reflect their routine. No other leader analyses missing features in memory drawings. |
| 3 | **C · Priya** | C labels each added feature with its document's date and origin. Mara uses remembered accounts, but she does not create this documentary source label. |
| 4 | **D · Niko** | D recruits people who have never visited before and prefers their first impressions to accounts from familiar residents. The statement paraphrases this selection rule. |
| 5 | **A · Mara** | A keeps conflicting recollections for discussion instead of selecting a correct memory. Reusing A is allowed; assigning an unused letter merely to make the answers different would ignore the instruction. |

**Review task, ungraded:** explain the difference between items 2 and5. Both concern Mara, but one is about omission in a drawing and the other about disagreement between recollections. One correctly attributed statement receives one practice point; ungraded notes do not add points.
